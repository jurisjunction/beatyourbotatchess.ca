What the bot does today, as of 25 October 2026. Where something is only planned, we say so.

Play it on Lichess as pawnOfTeresa.

What is the point of it?

Tools for teachers. The work began as a program to help one student work through algebra: it asks rather than tells, and every word it says is checked against what it actually sees. Chess is where we test that idea in the open, against anyone who wants a game.

The bot on Lichess is the research side: a player in a past master's style, with a person's limits, that gets to know you as an opponent. A Teacher mode is in development. It is meant to be set up by a human teacher and to stay under that teacher, never in their place. In the version being tested now, a student uses it directly.

Does it use a language model?

No. A language model chooses none of its moves and writes none of its words. Everything it says in the chat is a sentence written in advance and filled in with what it saw. That is why it can sound like an old chat bot.

Plainer wording is being worked on. When it comes, it will be labelled as a model's wording, checked against what the bot actually saw, and off unless you turn it on.

How did it learn to play like Morphy?

From Morphy's own games, and nothing else of his. In each position he faced, it learned which of the moves it can see he tended to choose.

Today that is 211 of his games, the regular ones in a public collection. His full record is about 400 games, but some 150 are games in which he gave odds, and it will never learn from those. The forty or so regular games it lacks are the next step. It did not study millions of games, and it did not learn by playing itself.

How does it choose a move?

Much as a club player does, only more methodically. It looks at every legal move one move deep for each side, and two or three moves deep at the few it likes best. Written rules set aside the moves that lose material for nothing. Morphy's school will give material for an attack on the king, for time, or for open lines, and so will the bot.

Among the moves left, it chooses as Morphy tended to, weighing about fifty named features of a move: development, the centre, open files, pressure on the king, and so on. It remembers its last few moves, so it keeps to what it was doing, and it has only so much thought for a whole game, so it tires.

How close is it to Morphy?

It is measured on games of his it never learned from. There, choosing as it does on Lichess, it picks the move Morphy played about 47% of the time; in the nine positions in ten where its rules allow his move, about half. In a typical position it is choosing among ten to twenty-five moves its rules allow, out of thirty to forty legal ones. Picking among those at random would get about nine in a hundred. Its Steinitz bot, coming next, picks Steinitz's move about 40% of the time on the same terms, 36% counting every move.

That is closeness of taste, not of strength. Judged by Stockfish on the same positions, its mistakes are about three times the size of Morphy's, and about the size of a strong club player's. In the openings Morphy played, it is about as good as he was. It sees a few moves ahead; Morphy saw much further.

Is it like Maia?

In aim, a little: both are meant to play like people, not like engines. In how, not much. Maia is a neural network trained on millions of Lichess games to play like players of a given rating.

This bot learned one master's taste from about two hundred of his games. It chooses only among moves that rules you could read aloud call sound, and it looks just a few moves ahead. That is why it can tell you why it played a move.

Maia is good at it, too. On Morphy's own games, Maia's strongest model, which never saw him, picks his move a little more often than this bot does: about 50% against 47%. Where this bot differs is in what it is made of and what it keeps. The Maia bots on Lichess play everyone alike. This bot keeps a picture of each opponent, read off their moves against it, and everything in that picture is a named count or weight that can be printed and read, not the inside of a network.

Did it read books about Morphy?

Not yet, as far as its play goes. Books about him, such as Frisco Del Rosario's A First Book of Morphy, are used in our research notes only. What it says in public chat comes from the game scores alone.

The opening theory of his day, which he would have known, is still to be added.

Does it get to know me?

Yes, from your moves against it and nothing else. As you play, it keeps a picture of you: which wins on the board you took and which you passed, when you gave material away, how far ahead your moves seem to look, and what you like to play, such as captures, checks or trading queens. Type what do you know about me and it tells you.

It uses that picture in one way. Among the moves it would be happy to play anyway, it prefers the ones where your likely replies go wrong. It never plays an unsound move in the hope that you miss something. Your first moves count for little; the picture firms up over a game.

Type remember me and it also reads your earlier games with it back from Lichess at the start of each game, taking their moves in a few at a time, so its picture of you starts well ahead of a stranger's. Type forget me and from your next game it stops. It never looks at your games against anyone else.

After a game it may offer a review: it asks what you were looking at on one or two of your moves, and keeps your answer, as one of six set phrases, in a study on Lichess you can open. It takes your word for those moves from then on.

Will it learn to play like me?

No. It stays in its master's style. What changes as you play is its picture of you, and with it which of its own moves it chooses against you.

What does it keep about me?

Its record of the games it plays: the moves, the result and Lichess's public id for each game. The record never holds your name, though the id leads to the game on Lichess, which shows both players. If you asked it to remember you, it follows you on Lichess until you say forget me.

Its picture of you is not saved: it is built again from the games each time. Between moves of a correspondence game it keeps a few notes about the game itself, such as whether you asked it to explain its moves, but never your name or your words, and it deletes them when the game ends.

The program that runs it keeps a working log on our machine for thirty days. It shows usernames and what the bot itself says. What you type is logged only as the word the bot took it for, never your own words. The privacy page has the whole list.

Is the plan it tells me the reason for its move?

Partly. When it explains a move, it names the two plainest reasons that counted for it and the moves it passed over: a readout of its scores, not a story made up afterwards. It does not yet say when the main reason was that Morphy played the move here himself, or when its picture of you tipped the choice.

It also names what it is playing for, such as the centre or your king, and what the move did for that. But that aim does not yet choose the move. In testing on Morphy's games, letting it steer made the bot a little less like Morphy, so for now the aim is shown and not used.

Does the clock matter?

Yes. It keeps a human clock. It plans its time move by move as a person would: quick in the opening, longer when the position is tense, quick again when there is little to choose, and it pauses before moving so as not to answer at machine speed. Short of time, it looks less far ahead at fewer moves.

It also has only so much thought for a whole game, set by the time control, and it tires as that runs down: it looks less far and considers fewer moves. So clock strategy is fair play. A long game, with tense positions that make it think hard, tires it, and low on time it is easier to trick.

In correspondence there is no clock, but it still tires over a long game, just as it would in one sitting.

Can I play it by correspondence?

Yes. It plays up to a hundred correspondence games at once and answers your move usually within a minute or two. If you wrote in the chat while it was away, it reads that before it moves.

Which players will there be?

For now, only players who have died, from games whose scores are in the public domain. Morphy is the first. Wilhelm Steinitz is next, learned from 583 of his games, and you will be able to choose which year's Steinitz you face.

A bot of a living player is planned, not built. It would be made only with their consent, signed with their own key, and would stop when they withdraw it.

Can I beat it?

That is the point. It looks a few moves ahead and no further, and it tires as a game goes on (see the clock). Beating it means you saw further than a bot in Morphy's style, not that you would have beaten Morphy.

It plays against its picture of you, but only among moves it would play anyway, so surprising it is a fair weapon: a move its picture did not expect makes the picture less sure of you.

Is it finished?

No. It is a research version, still being tested. This page says what it does today, and where something is only planned, it says so.
